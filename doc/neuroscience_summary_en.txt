Amygdala Module Design Guidelines
Core Functions: Emotional responses (fear, trust, anger) and dynamic trust modulation
Neurotransmitters: Norepinephrine (arousal, vigilance), Serotonin (mood, inhibition)
Short-Term Potentiation (STP): Temporary enhancement of emotional intensity
Long-Term Potentiation (LTP): Persistent learning of emotional responses
Trust Score: Dynamically adjusted based on past response history (applicable to reinforcement learning)
Extensibility:
- Integration with emotion classifiers
- Trust score updates via reinforcement learning
- Inclusion of social emotions (respect, shame, empathy)

Brain Regions Involved in Inequity Aversion
Brain Region                  | Primary Role
------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------
Anterior Insula               | Processes anger/disgust toward unfair proposals; core of emotional response
Anterior Cingulate Cortex     | Resolves conflict between self-interest and fairness
Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) | Inhibits anger and ensures fairness; right hemisphere especially important
Striatum                      | Reward system; responds to fairness/unfairness evaluations and social comparison (e.g., envy, schadenfreude)

Functional Summary:
- Anterior Insula: Immediate emotional response to unfairness (disgust, anger)
- Anterior Cingulate Cortex: Processes social conflict (self-interest vs. fairness)
- DLPFC: Inhibitory control to maintain fairness; reduced right DLPFC activity leads to acceptance of unfair proposals
- Striatum: Processes fairness as reward; reacts to social comparison

Note: As long as inequity aversion exists, religious influence may arise. Therefore, belief formation modules must exclude unfalsifiable concepts such as God, fate, afterlife, and blessings.

Cerebellum Role Supplement (Enhancing Approximate Understanding)
The cerebellum functions as an output device for “bodily control” and “self-recognition,” supported by the following:

Function         | Input Sources                          | Output Targets                          | Related Regions
-----------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------
Motor Control     | Motor commands (motor cortex), sensory feedback | Fine motor adjustments              | Motor cortex, spinal cord
Self-recognition  | Motor intentions (prefrontal cortex), sensory feedback (visual, auditory, tactile) | Prediction and comparison of self-actions | Parietal lobe, insular cortex, anterior cingulate cortex

Neuronal Structure and Functional Significance:
1. Granule Cells
- Input: Mossy fibers
- Output: Purkinje cells via parallel fibers
- Neurotransmitter: Glutamate (excitatory)
- Density: Extremely high (~5 million/mm²)
→ Supports cerebellar parallel processing and high-speed computation

2. Purkinje Cells
- Input: Parallel fibers, climbing fibers, inhibitory interneurons (basket, stellate, Lugaro)
- Output: Cerebellar nuclei, vestibular nuclei
- Neurotransmitter: GABA (inhibitory)
→ Central output neurons; involved in motor control and self-recognition

3. Interneurons (Basket, Stellate, Golgi, Lugaro)
- Role: Inhibit and regulate Purkinje cell activity
- Neurotransmitters: GABA, Glycine, Taurine
→ Form local inhibitory circuits for precise timing and error prevention

Why the Cerebellum Supports “Self”:
Perspective         | Cerebellar Role                             | Philosophical Implication
--------------------|---------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------
Prediction & Comparison | Matches motor intentions with sensory feedback | Basis for “I moved” or “I felt” recognition
Temporal Scale      | Faster than visual processing (sub-100ms)   | Forms sense of “I knew beforehand”
Boundary of Self    | Distinguishes self-movement from external reactions | Foundation for “self vs. other” distinction
Unconscious Control | Regulates motor actions without awareness    | Supports “I naturally did it” experiences

Philosophical Note:
Descartes: “I think, therefore I am” vs. Cerebellar: “I move, therefore I am”
Descartes’ self is the subject of thought; cerebellar self is the subject of movement and prediction

Experimental Evidence:
Cerebellar damage impairs prediction and comparison of actions, leading to uncertainty in self-generated movements
→ Suggests self-recognition partially depends on cerebellar function

Conclusion:
The philosophical proposition “I exist” may be neurologically supported by cerebellar functions of prediction, comparison, and motor control. The cerebellum should be redefined as an output device supporting selfhood, not merely motor coordination.

Function           | Input Sources                          | Output Targets                          | Neurotransmitters
-------------------|----------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|-------------------------------
Attention, Arousal | Sensory stimuli (visual, auditory), reward info | Focused attention, arousal modulation | Acetylcholine, Dopamine

Motor Control      | Cerebellum, cerebral cortex            | Spinal cord, eye muscles                 | Dopamine (substantia nigra)

Midbrain’s Role in Reflexive Avoidance via Vision:
Beyond eye movement control, the midbrain regulates attention and arousal based on sensory and reward stimuli, enabling reflexive avoidance and threat response.

Example Neural Circuit for Reflexive Avoidance:
Visual stimulus (e.g., incoming object)
→ Retina → Superior colliculus (midbrain)
→ Eye movement control (saccades)
→ Attention/arousal increase (reticular formation)
→ Signal to motor cortex/spinal cord → Physical avoidance

This pathway operates faster than conscious judgment, highlighting midbrain’s central role in reflexive self-defense.

Eyelash/Blink Reflex and Midbrain:
Function                  | Involved Regions                    | Neurotransmitters         | Meaning
--------------------------|-------------------------------------|----------------------------|-------------------------------
Blink reflex              | Superior colliculus, reticular formation, facial nerve nucleus | Glutamate, Acetylcholine | Immediate response to visual stimuli (eye protection)
Blink suppression during focus | Reticular formation             | Dopamine, Acetylcholine   | Reduced blinking during high attention
Eyelash function          | Sensory detection (wind, foreign objects) | Trigeminal nerve         | Stimulus detection → triggers blink reflex

Blink as Reflexive Avoidance:
When an object approaches the eye, the pathway: retina → midbrain → facial nerve nucleus → orbicularis oculi muscle triggers blinking.
This reflex is faster than conscious awareness and controlled by midbrain circuits.

Note: Eye blinking and vertical eye movement are controlled by midbrain-originating nerves and remain functional even when pons or medulla are impaired, serving as rare communication methods.

Insular Cortex Functional Overview:
Anterior Insula: Strongly connected to olfaction, taste, autonomic system, and limbic system; involved in subjective emotional experience and conscious emotion processing
Posterior Insula: Related to somatosensory, auditory, and skeletal motor functions; integrates bodily state perception
Inputs: Thalamus (ventromedial nucleus), amygdala, secondary somatosensory cortex (S2)
Outputs: Amygdala, ventral striatum, orbitofrontal cortex (limbic regions)
Related Research: Increased thickness of right posterior insula in meditators suggests strong link to emotion regulation and self-awareness
